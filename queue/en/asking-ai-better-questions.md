Most people who use AI assistants know the feeling: sometimes the answer is surprisingly good, and sometimes it is generic and useless. A large part of that difference comes not from the model but from the question. Working with language models, one problem I see often is that people talk to an assistant as if it were a colleague who has known them for years, rather than a stranger who knows nothing about them. This is not a technical "prompt engineering" guide. It is a practical handbook for anyone who uses ChatGPT, Claude, Gemini or any other assistant in daily life, with ten before-and-after examples in a table at the end.

## The assistant does not know you

Unless you tell it, an AI assistant does not know who you are, why you are asking or what you will do with the answer. Ask it to "write a complaint letter" and it has to guess who it is for, what it is about, what tone you want and how long it should be. What it guesses is usually an "average" letter.

Anthropic's prompting guide for Claude offers a handy test: imagine showing your request to a colleague who has minimal context on the task. Could they follow it, or would they need to ask lots of questions? If a person would be confused, the model probably will be too.

## The parts of a good request

You will not need all of these every time, but requests that work well often include them. Google's prompting guide for Gemini suggests a similar framework: persona, task, context and format.

- **Context:** Who are you, and what is the situation? "I run a small bakery", "I teach secondary school", "I'm renting a flat for the first time".
- **Goal:** What will you use the answer for? Making a decision, sending it to someone, understanding something?
- **Format:** What should the output look like? A bulleted list, a table, a 150-word email, step-by-step instructions?
- **Example:** If you have an example you like, include it. "Write it in this tone" usually works better than describing the tone.
- **Constraints:** What should be avoided? "No jargon", "keep the budget under 200 pounds", "no more than one page".

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> If you are not sure what you want, say so: "Before you answer, ask me the questions you need to understand my situation." The assistant can help you fill in the gaps.</div>

## Ask for sources, then check them

AI assistants sometimes state wrong information with total confidence, and can even invent articles, legal provisions or links that do not exist. On anything important:

- **Ask for sources:** "What is this based on? List official sources."
- **Open the links:** Check yourself that each link exists and actually supports the claim.
- **Ask about uncertainty:** "Which parts of this are you unsure about?" brings weak spots into view.
- **Question freshness:** A model's knowledge stops at a certain date. For prices, regulations, elections or exchange rates, check a current official source.

In health, law and finance, AI assistants can help you understand a topic, but they are no substitute for professional advice. This post is general information too.

## Have a conversation, not a one-shot

The first answer does not have to be perfect. As OpenAI's help page for ChatGPT also stresses, good results usually come from a loop of trying, reviewing the output and refining the request. When you do not like an answer, rather than starting over, say what is missing:

- "Shorter, please: five bullet points at most."
- "That's too formal; use a friendly tone."
- "Explain the second point with an example."
- "Rewrite this as if explaining it to a ten-year-old."

Breaking big tasks into steps also helps: first an outline, then each section, then a final review of the whole.

## Ten before-and-after examples

| # | Before | After | What was added? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | "Write a diet plan." | "I'm 35, work at a desk and walk three days a week. Give me a week of quick, practical breakfast ideas. I'll see a doctor for any health issues." | Context, limits, expectations |
| 2 | "Write an email." | "Write a polite but firm email to my landlord saying the boiler has been broken for 10 days and asking when this week a repair can be arranged. Maximum 120 words." | Recipient, goal, tone, length |
| 3 | "Summarise this." | "Summarise the report below in 5 bullet points for my manager. One sentence each, plus a one-sentence recommendation at the end." | Reader, format |
| 4 | "Suggest a holiday." | "Suggest 3 four-day trips in May that work without a car, for two adults, with a budget of 400 euros per person excluding accommodation. Put them in a table." | Dates, constraints, budget, format |
| 5 | "How much can my rent go up?" | "Explain in general terms how legal limits on residential rent increases are set where I live, and tell me which official source I should check for the current figure." | Pointer to an official, current source |
| 6 | "Teach me Excel." | "I've never used a formula in Excel. I want to total my monthly spending and split it by category. Walk me through it step by step with sample data." | Skill level, concrete goal |
| 7 | "Is this true?" | "Is the claim below true? Say which sources you are relying on, and list separately anything you are unsure about." | Sources and uncertainty |

## Think before you share

Context helps, but you do not have to share everything. Think twice before typing national ID numbers, passwords, bank details, other people's personal data or confidential work documents into a general-purpose assistant. You can often ask the same question with names and details changed ("Company A", "client X"). Check your service's settings to see how conversations are stored and whether they are used to improve models.

<div class="callout"><strong>Note:</strong> Responsibility for how an answer is used lies with the person using it. Always give a final read to anything you will send to others or base a decision on.</div>

<div class="faq"><details><summary>Is a longer prompt always better?</summary><p>No. The goal is clarity, not length. A one-line question is fine for simple things. Adding context and a goal next to "please" makes a bigger difference.</p></details><details><summary>Can an assistant say "I don't know"?</summary><p>It can, but it does not always do so.
