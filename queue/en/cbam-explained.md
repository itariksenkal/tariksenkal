For a Turkish company selling steel, aluminium, cement or fertiliser to the European Union, 2026 is the year carbon stopped being only an environmental topic and became a direct trade cost. The EU's **Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism** (CBAM; in Turkish, SKDM) entered its definitive phase on 1 January 2026. It is often called a "carbon tax" in the press, but it works in a somewhat more detailed way. In this post I outline what CBAM is, what the simplifications adopted in 2025 changed, what exporters need to prepare, and how it connects to Türkiye's own Climate Law.

<div class="callout"><strong>Note:</strong> This post is general information, not legal or financial advice. For your own situation, rely on current legislation and expert advice.</div>

## What is CBAM and why does it exist?

The EU prices emissions from its own industry through the **EU Emissions Trading System** (EU ETS): polluting installations must surrender an allowance for every tonne of CO₂ they emit. That creates two risks:

1. Production shifting to countries without a carbon price (**carbon leakage**).
2. EU producers being at a competitive disadvantage against imports that carry no carbon cost.

CBAM is designed to reduce both. The logic is simple: for certain goods imported into the EU, a carbon price similar to the one paid by EU producers is applied to the emissions released in making them (embedded emissions). The price is paid through **CBAM certificates** that the importer buys and surrenders, with certificate prices linked to EU ETS prices.

The mechanism runs in parallel with the gradual phase-out of free allowances that some sectors receive under the EU ETS. In other words, the CBAM burden will grow in the coming years as free allocation shrinks.

## Which sectors are covered?

According to the European Commission, CBAM covers:

- Cement
- Iron and steel
- Aluminium
- Fertilisers
- Electricity
- Hydrogen

These sectors were chosen because they are carbon-intensive and face a relatively high risk of carbon leakage. Covered goods are defined by customs tariff codes, so the first step in checking whether a product is in scope is its tariff code.

## Transitional versus definitive period

CBAM came in two stages:

| Period | Timing | What is required? |
|---|---|---|
| Transitional | October 2023 – December 2025 | Reporting only: importers reported embedded emissions quarterly. No financial obligation. |
| Definitive | From 1 January 2026 | Authorisation, annual declaration and certificate surrender. The carbon price is actually paid. |

The transitional phase was a learning period for both companies and the EU on data collection and calculation methods.

## What did the 2025 simplifications change?

In October 2025 the EU adopted **Regulation (EU) 2025/2083**, which simplifies the CBAM rules. It was published in the Official Journal on 17 October 2025 and entered into force on 20 October 2025. The key changes:

- **A 50-tonne threshold:** The old EUR 150-per-consignment exemption was replaced by a cumulative annual threshold of 50 tonnes per importer. It covers iron and steel, aluminium, fertilisers and cement, but not electricity or hydrogen. If the threshold is exceeded, all of that year's imports fall in scope.
- **Authorised CBAM declarant:** Importers expecting to exceed the threshold must apply for authorisation in advance. Those who applied by 31 March 2026 may keep importing while a decision is pending.
- **Annual declaration:** Due by 30 September of the year after import. The first deadline, for 2026 imports, is 30 September 2027.
- **Certificate sales:** Certificates will be sold through a central platform from 1 February 2027. Certificates bought in 2027 for 2026 imports are priced on the 2026 quarterly averages of EU ETS prices.
- **Quarterly holding:** The share of certificates that must be held at the end of each quarter was cut from 80% to 50%.
- **Carbon price paid abroad:** A carbon price effectively paid in the country of origin can be deducted from the number of certificates to surrender.

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> Legally, the obligation sits with the EU importer, but the emissions data the importer needs come from your plant. Expect customers to ask for verifiable, product-level emissions data, and exporters who prepare early will also be in a stronger negotiating position.</div>

## What should Turkish exporters do?

Concrete preparation steps can be summarised as follows:

1. **Clarify scope:** Check your products' tariff codes and work out your volumes going to the EU.
2. **Build installation-level emissions data:** Calculate direct emissions per product (and indirect emissions where required). Also track emissions from precursor materials that are themselves in scope.
3. **Prepare for monitoring and verification:** Actual emission values must be verified by an accredited verifier to be used. Without actual data, default values apply, and the rules generally set these conservatively, so failing to provide data usually means a higher cost.
4. **Talk to your customer:** Find out what format and timeline your EU buyer expects, and add data-provision and cost-sharing clauses to contracts.
5. **Factor emission cuts into costs:** Energy efficiency, renewable electricity and process improvements now translate directly into competitiveness.

## Türkiye's Climate Law and the ETS link

Türkiye's **Climate Law No. 7552** was adopted on 2 July 2025 and published in the Official Gazette, entering into force, on 9 July 2025. It contains three elements that matter for CBAM:

- **A national Emissions Trading System:** The law provides for an ETS under which covered installations surrender allowances each year for their verified emissions. The market is to be operated by Türkiye's energy market operator, EPİAŞ (Enerji Piyasaları İşletme A.Ş.).
- **A pilot period:** Full implementation is preceded by a pilot phase whose scope, duration and procedures are set by the Carbon Market Board; administrative fines are reduced by 80% during the pilot.
- **Option for a national CBAM:** The law allows a border carbon adjustment to be set up for embedded emissions of goods imported into Türkiye.

Because EU CBAM allows deduction of a carbon price effectively paid in the country of origin, a functioning carbon price in Türkiye could, in principle, mean that part of this cost stays at home. How far that holds in practice depends on the Turkish ETS design, price level and free allocation. These details are worth following as they become clearer.
