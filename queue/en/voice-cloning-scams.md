The phone rings, and the voice on the other end belongs to your child, your sibling or your manager. They sound panicked, something has gone wrong, and money needs to be sent right now. The voice is so familiar that suspicion never crosses your mind. AI voice cloning has made this scenario easier for scammers than ever before. In this post I explain how these scams work, which signals should make you stop, and one of the most effective defences: a family code word. Having worked with speech and language technology, I can say this plainly: recognising a voice is no longer proof that it is real.

## How voice cloning works

Voice cloning is AI technology that uses samples of a person’s voice to generate sentences they never said, in their voice. It has legitimate uses, such as audiobooks, dubbing and speech support for people who have lost their voice. The same technology can also be abused.

In a consumer alert published in March 2023, the US Federal Trade Commission (FTC) noted that all a scammer needs is a short audio clip of your family member’s voice and a voice-cloning program. That clip can come from a video posted online, a social media story or a brief earlier phone call.

## The most common scenarios

### “A relative in trouble”

This is the best-known version. The caller speaks in the voice of a grandchild, child or close relative: they have been in a car accident, they have been arrested, they are stuck abroad. Sometimes they keep it short and hand the phone to a second person playing a “lawyer” or “police officer”. The common thread is an immediate request for money and a plea not to tell anyone.

### “The boss is calling” (CEO fraud)

In business, scammers imitate an executive’s voice to ask employees for an urgent payment, a transfer for a confidential acquisition or sensitive information. Impersonation is not limited to voice. In a case that came to light in Hong Kong in 2024, affecting British engineering firm Arup, an employee was persuaded by a video call featuring deepfakes of colleagues and transferred about HK$200 million. In May 2025, the FBI also warned that since April 2025 people had been targeted with AI-generated voice messages impersonating senior US officials.

### Celebrity and official impersonation

Similar methods have been seen elsewhere. In Türkiye, for example, a July 2024 warning from the national police’s cybercrime department, reported in the press, described interviews with well-known figures being altered with AI, imitating their faces and voices, to promote fake investment schemes.

## Red flags

However convincing the voice, the behavioural pattern of the scam is usually the same. Even one of these signs is reason enough to stop and think:

| Red flag | Why it should worry you |
|---|---|
| Extreme urgency | “Right now” and “or it will be too late” are meant to stop you thinking |
| Request for secrecy | “Don’t tell anyone, don’t worry Mum and Dad” is designed to prevent you from checking |
| Unusual payment method | Cryptocurrency, gift cards, wire transfers to unfamiliar accounts, cash handed to a courier |
| Unknown number | “My phone broke, I’m calling from a friend’s phone” |
| Short, one-way call | The call is handed to someone else before you can ask questions |
| Style that doesn’t fit | Forms of address, word choice or tone that do not match the person you know |

The FTC also stresses that scammers ask for payment methods that make it hard to get your money back: wire transfers, cryptocurrency, or gift card numbers and PINs.

## A family code word: simple but effective

In its December 2024 announcement focused on fraud using generative AI, the FBI recommended that families agree on **a secret word or phrase that only they know** to verify each other’s identity.

It is easy to put into practice:

- Sit down with your family and choose a word or short phrase nobody could guess. Avoid anything findable on social media, such as a pet’s name or a birthplace.
- Share it in person, not by text message.
- Make the rule clear: anyone asking for money or sensitive information in an emergency must know the code word, whoever they claim to be.
- Talk this through especially with older relatives, who are often the target of “your grandchild is in trouble” calls.

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> The same logic works at the office. Make it a rule that any payment or transfer instruction received by phone or voice message is always confirmed through a second channel, such as a call back to a known number or written internal approval.</div>

## What to do if you get such a call

1. **Stay calm and buy time.** Saying “I’ll call you right back” is perfectly legitimate. A real loved one will understand.
2. **Hang up and call back yourself.** This is the shared advice of the FTC and FBI: call the person on a number you already know is theirs. If you cannot reach them, contact another family member or a friend. If the caller claims to represent a bank or organisation, look up the official number yourself.
3. **Ask for the code word.** If you have one, ask for it.
4. **Ask a question only they could answer.** Something about a shared memory that is not on social media.
5. **Don’t send money.** Never send wire transfers, crypto, gift cards or cash for a request you cannot verify. As a general reminder, real police officers and banks do not ask you to transfer money or reveal passwords over the phone.
6. **Report it.** If you sent money, call your bank immediately, then report the attempt to the police. In the US, the FTC accepts reports at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and the FBI at ic3.gov.

## Prevention

- **Share your voice less.** The FBI suggests keeping social media accounts private and limiting public posts of your voice and image.
- **Be careful with unknown numbers.** Avoid long conversations with callers you don’t know; any recorded conversation could become a sample used to imitate you.
- **Talk to your family.** Perhaps the most useful thing you can do after reading this is to share these scenarios with the people close to you, especially older relatives.
