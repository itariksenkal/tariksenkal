Showing a few percentage points of improvement in a paper and building a product someone will pay for every month draw on the same expertise, but they are very different jobs. Many strong researchers in Türkiye, especially in fields like AI, realise that their work could create value outside the lab and start thinking about founding a company. The questions that matter in this transition differ from the ones academia trains us to ask: "Who does this help?" instead of "Is it novel?", and "Will the user pay?" instead of "What will the reviewers say?" In this post I look at the path from paper to product, validating with users, the rules on university inventions, funding programmes and the skills worth learning.

<div class="callout"><strong>Note:</strong> The sections on intellectual property and university regulations are general information, not legal advice. For your own situation, talk to your university's technology transfer office and a specialist.</div>

## The gap between a paper and a product

An academic paper usually shows that a method beats earlier methods on a particular dataset. A product has to solve a real user's real problem, under real conditions, reliably and sustainably. The difference can be summed up like this:

| In academia | In a product |
|---|---|
| Novelty and originality | Value delivered to the user |
| Success on standard benchmarks | Consistency on messy, real data |
| One-off experiment | A system that runs and is maintained continuously |
| Peer review | The customer's buying decision |
| The best result | A result that is good enough, fast and affordable |

None of this means research is worthless; deep technical knowledge is an advantage that is hard to copy. But turning that knowledge into a product takes extra work: letting users define the problem.

## Validate with users first

The most common mistake researchers make is spending months perfecting the technology before talking to users. The healthier path is the reverse: find the people who have the problem you think your technology can solve, and talk to them.

The U.S. National Science Foundation's **I-Corps** programme is a well-known example of this approach: a seven-week experiential training programme designed to help scientists and engineers move their research towards commercialisation. The shared idea behind such programmes is to get out of the lab and hold systematic conversations with potential customers.

A few things to keep in mind in those conversations:

-   Ask how people do the job today instead of pitching your product.
-   "Would you use this?" gets misleading answers; "When did you last spend money or time on this problem, and how much?" is more revealing.
-   Distinguish the person who feels the problem from the person who makes the purchase.
-   Take notes and look for patterns across interviews; one enthusiastic conversation is not evidence.

## Intellectual property and university rules

If your research was done at a university, the university may have rights over the resulting invention. In Türkiye this is governed mainly by **Article 121 of the Industrial Property Law No. 6769**. Under the law, the main points are:

-   For inventions arising from scientific work at higher education institutions, the rules on employee inventions apply, subject to the article's specific provisions.
-   The inventor must notify the university of the invention in writing and without delay.
-   If the university claims rights to the invention, it must file a patent application; otherwise the invention becomes a "free invention".
-   At least one third of the income from the invention goes to the inventor.
-   For work done under a specific contract with other institutions, ownership is determined primarily by the contract.

Article 122 also requires inventions arising from **publicly funded projects** to be reported to the funding body. In AI the picture can be more complex: software and models are not always protected by patents but by copyright or as trade secrets, and the licence terms of training data can also affect commercialisation.

The first stop for these questions is usually the university's **Technology Transfer Office (TTO)**. TTOs supported by TÜBİTAK's 1513 programme help with identifying and protecting intellectual property, licensing, incubation and accelerator services for spin-offs, and university–industry collaboration.

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> Talk to your TTO before you publish. Disclosing an invention in a publication can make a later patent difficult. A short early conversation prevents uncertainty that could put both you and the university in an awkward position later.</div>

## Funding routes

In Türkiye, the main public support for research-based startups sits among TÜBİTAK's industry support programmes. The best-known early-stage example is **BiGG**: running since 2012, it now appears in TÜBİTAK's programme list as **1812 Investment-Based Entrepreneurship Support Programme (BiGG Yatırım)**. According to TÜBİTAK's programme page, the structure is roughly:

-   **Stage 1 (accelerator):** implementing organisations collect idea applications and help founders prepare business plans through training, mentoring and pre-incubation. Students from associate to doctoral level, and graduates, can apply.
-   **Stage 2 (pre-seed):** successful business plans lead to a company, and TÜBİTAK's BiGG fund can invest in exchange for up to 5% equity.
-   **Stage 3 (seed):** companies can seek investment from TÜBİTAK-backed seed funds for up to 10% equity.

Investment amounts and deadlines are announced per call, so check the current call text. Once a company exists, R&D support for SMEs (for example the 1507 SME R&D Start-up Support Programme) and university–industry collaboration programmes are also options. Technopark incubation centres and private investors are important sources as well.

## Skills to learn

The hardest parts of the move from researcher to founder are usually not technical:

1.  **Selling and talking to customers:** understanding a problem and explaining the value of a solution in the other person's language.
2.  **Prioritisation:** shipping what is good enough on time rather than what is perfect.
3.  **Building a team:** valuing non-technical roles (sales, operations, finance) and finding complementary co-founders.
4.  **Financial literacy:** cash flow, unit costs and pricing.
5.  **Engineering discipline:** turning research code into a maintainable, tested product.

An academic background also brings real advantages: systematic thinking, comfort with uncertainty, evidence-based decisions and deep domain knowledge. The task is to point those strengths at the user's problem.
