Many people's story with AI tools goes the same way: a burst of enthusiastic questions in the first few days, then the tool sits forgotten in a browser tab. The problem is rarely that the tool is not capable. It is that we do not know where in our daily work to put it. Used in the right places, AI can noticeably cut the time spent reading a long report, writing a difficult email or planning the week. In this post I share concrete everyday uses, what the research says, the situations to avoid, and a simple routine you can adapt to your own week.

## What does the research say?

One of the most cited studies on AI in knowledge work is the experiment by Shakked Noy and Whitney Zhang of MIT, published in Science in 2023. They gave 444 college-educated professionals writing tasks specific to their occupations. In the group using ChatGPT, average time fell by 40% and the quality score given by evaluators rose by 18%. The researchers note that factual accuracy was not separately checked.

In a study of 5,179 customer support agents by Brynjolfsson, Li and Raymond (Stanford and MIT), AI assistance raised the number of issues resolved per hour by 14% on average. The gain reached 34% for newer and less-skilled agents, while the effect on experienced agents was minimal.

Another experiment, run with consultants by researchers from Harvard Business School and BCG, adds an important caution: AI markedly improved performance on some tasks, yet on a task chosen to lie outside its capabilities, users became less likely to reach the correct answer, even though the tasks looked similarly difficult. The researchers call this the "jagged frontier". In short, AI is very good at some tasks, not all, and knowing which is which is the user's job.

## Five concrete uses

**1. Summarising long documents.** Before reading a 60-page report or contract, you can ask: "List the main findings, the actions that fall to me, and the risks I should watch for." A summary works best not as a replacement for reading, but as a way to find which parts deserve careful reading.

**2. Drafting emails.** For a difficult email (a refusal, a delay notice, a reminder), first write the core message yourself in two or three bullet points, then ask for a draft with a tone: "Polite but clear, 120 words at most." Always read and send the final version yourself.

**3. Meeting notes.** Turning a transcript or messy notes into a summary of "decisions, owners, dates" saves a lot of time. Remember to get participants' consent before recording and to check your company's policy.

**4. Research with sources.** Tools that search the web and cite sources are handy for getting into a topic quickly. But click through and verify every critical claim yourself; summaries that misrepresent their sources are not rare.

**5. Planning.** Listing your tasks and asking "Group these by urgency and importance and suggest a realistic weekly plan with no more than two deep-work blocks per day" is a good way to break the inertia of a blank page.

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> The easiest way to get better results is to give context: who you are, who the text is for, the length and the tone. "Turn this into a 150-word, non-technical summary for a senior manager" produces a very different result from "fix this".</div>

## What to avoid

-   **Sharing sensitive data:** customer information, personal data, health information and company secrets should not go into tools that your organisation has not approved and whose data terms you do not know.
-   **Sending without checking:** an AI-written email goes out under your name; a wrong date or name is your mistake.
-   **Trusting unsourced claims:** do not use unverified output for figures, quotes or regulations.
-   **Making decisions about people:** using AI as the sole decision-maker for hiring or performance reviews carries ethical and legal risks.
-   **Handing over everything:** outsourcing the work you should be thinking through yourself (strategy, hard feedback, a personal message) brings shallowness, not productivity.

## A simple weekly routine

The best way to turn AI into a lasting habit is to tie it to small, recurring moments. Here is a starting point:

| When | What | Example prompt |
|---|---|---|
| Monday morning (15 min) | Weekly plan | "Here is what I need to do this week… Prioritise it and spread it across the days." |
| Every day (5–10 min) | Summary of long documents or threads | "Pull out the actions that fall to me from this text." |
| After meetings (5 min) | Tidy the notes | "Summarise as decisions, owners and dates." |
| Wednesday (20 min) | Sourced background research on one topic | "Find current official sources on this topic and summarise them with links." |
| Friday afternoon (15 min) | Weekly review | "Here is what I finished and what is left. Give me three suggestions for next week." |

Running this routine for a month and noting which steps genuinely save time is the most reliable way to find the uses that suit you.
