A tool that can write an assignment in seconds is both a huge opportunity and a real trap for students. The same chat assistant can act like a patient private tutor, or like a shortcut that quietly takes over the thinking and, with it, the learning. The difference lies less in the tool than in how we use it. In this post I look at how to use AI to actually learn rather than to cheat: how to prompt it like a tutor, how to check its answers, how to disclose your use, and what higher education in Türkiye has said about it so far.

## Shortcut, crutch or tutor?

One of the most instructive studies on this question was carried out in Türkiye. A paper by Hamsa Bastani and colleagues, published in PNAS in 2025, is based on a field experiment in maths classes at a large Turkish high school during the autumn 2023 term. Some students got an unrestricted ChatGPT-style assistant; others got an assistant configured to behave like a tutor, giving hints instead of handing over answers.

The results are striking. During practice, both groups performed markedly better. But on the exam, taken without the tool, students who had used the unrestricted assistant did worse than students who never had access; the researchers attribute this to students using the tool as a "crutch". Students who used the tutor-style assistant scored statistically about the same as the control group, so the harm was largely avoided.

The lesson is simple: when AI gives you the answer, you have finished the exercise, but you have not finished learning.

## Prompts that turn the tool into a tutor

The easiest way to turn an "answer machine" into a tutor is to say clearly what you want from it. A few examples:

-   "Don't solve this for me. Give me step-by-step hints and wait for my attempt at each step."
-   "Explain this topic as you would to a 15-year-old, then ask me three questions to check that I understood."
-   "Is there a mistake in my solution below? Don't tell me what it is; just point me to the line I should look at again."
-   "I've summarised the main argument of this article in my own words. Is there anything I've missed or misunderstood?"
-   "I'm preparing for an exam. Write five questions on this unit, increasing in difficulty, and don't show the answers until I ask."

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> The best way to test whether you really understand something is to explain it to the AI yourself and ask it to find the gaps in your explanation. Reversing the roles turns passive reading into active learning.</div>

## Why and how to check the answers

Language models write fluently, but not always correctly. Be especially careful with:

-   **References:** models can invent articles and books or give wrong page numbers. The Turkish Council of Higher Education (YÖK) ethics guide also warns that AI can present fabricated sources as real during literature searches. Find every source yourself in a library database or search engine.
-   **Numbers and calculations:** redo the mathematical steps yourself; errors are common in multi-step work.
-   **Current information:** the model's knowledge may stop at a certain date; verify regulations, exam dates and similar facts at the official source.
-   **Local context:** models can make more mistakes on country-specific law, curricula or history.

A practical rule: before you put a claim into your assignment, make sure you have seen it in at least one reliable source that is independent of the AI.

## Disclosing your use and academic integrity

Using AI is not cheating in itself; cheating is presenting someone else's (or a tool's) work as your own. That is why transparency sits at the centre of everything.

In May 2024, YÖK published an "Ethics Guide on the Use of Generative AI in Scientific Research and Publication Activities of Higher Education Institutions". It emphasises principles such as transparency, honesty, care, fairness, confidentiality and accountability. A few points matter especially for students:

-   Failing to state that generative AI was used to produce content is listed among the situations that can lead to ethical problems, and the guide says the sections where AI was used should be explained in the methods section.
-   AI cannot be listed as an author, because it cannot take responsibility for the work the way a researcher can.
-   It may be used for translation or language editing, but checking the final text and taking responsibility for it remain with the author.

In November 2025, YÖK President Erol Özvar said a comprehensive regulation on AI use in theses and articles was on its way. Meanwhile, universities have started writing their own rules. For example, an amendment to Ege University's graduate regulation, published in the Official Gazette on 18 October 2026, requires master's and doctoral students to declare whether they used generative AI when submitting their thesis, and to follow YÖK's ethics guide. Other universities have published declaration forms and guidelines too.

In practice, a good disclosure covers which tool you used, for what purpose (brainstorming, language editing, summarising and so on) and in which part of the work. If your instructor or department has a set format, use it; otherwise a short, honest note is enough.

## What to avoid

-   **Having the whole assignment written for you:** it is an integrity breach, and it stops you from learning what you will face in the exam.
-   **Sharing personal or confidential information:** the YÖK guide also says personal data should not be entered into these systems unless it has been anonymised or masked.
-   **Using AI without checking the rules:** some courses allow it freely, others restrict it. Read the syllabus and ask if in doubt.
-   **Trusting "undetectable" promises:** the real risk is not getting caught; it is not having learned.
