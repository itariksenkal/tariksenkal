On some winter mornings a grey haze settles over the city and the weather app on your phone shows a small orange or red badge: "air quality unhealthy". What exactly is that number measuring, which pollutant is it looking at, and what is it asking us to do? Because of my environmental-engineering training and work on air-pollutant modelling, I get asked this a lot. In this post I explain the main pollutants, the logic of an air quality index, the World Health Organization's guideline values and, for readers in Türkiye, where to find official data.

## Why it matters

According to the World Health Organization (WHO), ambient air pollution was linked to an estimated **4.2 million** premature deaths worldwide in 2019. In the same year, **99%** of the world's population lived in places where WHO guideline levels were not met. Air pollution is not only a problem for big industrial cities; it concerns almost everyone.

The good news is that air quality is now measured hourly and the data are public. Once you know how to read them, you can use them for everyday decisions, such as whether to exercise outdoors.

## Five key pollutants

**PM2.5 (fine particulate matter):** Particles 2.5 micrometres across or smaller, many times thinner than a human hair. They can reach deep into the lungs. Sources include household heating, traffic, industry and agricultural burning. It is the pollutant health experts focus on most.

**PM10 (coarse particulate matter):** Particles 10 micrometres or smaller, which includes PM2.5. Additional sources include dust, construction, road dust and desert dust transport.

**NO₂ (nitrogen dioxide):** Mostly from combustion, especially road traffic. Levels are high near busy roads.

**O₃ (ground-level ozone):** It does not come out of a chimney. It forms when nitrogen oxides and volatile organic compounds react in sunlight. That is why it usually peaks on summer afternoons, and sometimes it is higher on the outskirts than in the city centre.

**SO₂ (sulphur dioxide):** Produced by burning sulphur-containing fuels, especially coal. It can stand out in winter where coal heating is common.

## How an air quality index works

Monitoring stations measure each pollutant in its own unit, usually micrograms per cubic metre (µg/m³). A table reading "PM2.5 38 µg/m³, NO₂ 54 µg/m³" means little to most people. An **air quality index (AQI)** converts these different measurements into one simple, colour-coded scale.

In the common approach, with the US Environmental Protection Agency's AQI as the best-known example, a sub-index is calculated for each pollutant and the **worst one** is reported as the overall index. So if a day looks "unhealthy", it is usually because of one pollutant, and it is worth checking which.

In Türkiye, data from the National Air Quality Monitoring Network of the Ministry of Environment, Urbanisation and Climate Change are published hourly via the e-Government portal and havaizleme.gov.tr, using these categories:

| Index value | Category (Turkish) | Rough meaning |
|---|---|---|
| 0–50 | İyi (Good) | Fine for everyone |
| 51–100 | Orta (Moderate) | Very sensitive people take care |
| 101–150 | Hassas (Sensitive) | Sensitive groups may be affected |
| 151–200 | Sağlıksız (Unhealthy) | Everyone may start to be affected |
| 201–300 | Kötü (Bad) | Serious health effects possible |
| 301–500 | Tehlikeli (Hazardous) | Emergency level |

<div class="callout"><strong>Tip:</strong> Look beyond the index number to see which pollutant is dominant. In winter it is usually particulate matter; on summer afternoons, often ozone. The pollutant involved changes which precautions make sense.</div>

## The WHO 2021 guideline values

In September 2021 the WHO updated its air quality guidelines for the first time in about 15 years and lowered many values significantly. These are not legal limits but evidence-based recommendations to protect health. National legal limits are often higher.

| Pollutant | Averaging time | WHO 2021 guideline |
|---|---|---|
| PM2.5 | Annual | 5 µg/m³ |
| PM2.5 | 24-hour | 15 µg/m³ |
| PM10 | Annual | 15 µg/m³ |
| PM10 | 24-hour | 45 µg/m³ |
| NO₂ | Annual | 10 µg/m³ |
| NO₂ | 24-hour | 25 µg/m³ |
| O₃ | 8-hour | 100 µg/m³ |
| O₃ | Peak season | 60 µg/m³ |
| SO₂ | 24-hour | 40 µg/m³ |

One thing to keep in mind: an app's index category and a WHO guideline value are not the same. The index may say "moderate" on a day when the 24-hour PM2.5 average is above the WHO's 15 µg/m³ recommendation.

## Practical guidance for bad-air days

What follows is general information; if you have a specific health condition, your doctor's advice comes first.

- **Sensitive groups first:** Children, older adults, pregnant people and those with asthma, COPD or heart disease are more vulnerable. They should take care even in the "sensitive" category.
- **Time outdoor exercise:** When the index is high, postpone strenuous exercise or move it indoors. With ozone pollution, early mornings are usually better.
- **Keep away from heavy traffic:** When walking or cycling, choose quieter streets over main roads.
- **Protect the indoors:** During high particle pollution, avoiding long periods with windows open and using suitably filtered air purifiers can reduce indoor exposure.
- **Cut your own contribution:** Using appropriate fuel and well-maintained stoves or boilers for home heating, and not burning waste outdoors, directly affect local pollution.

<div class="callout"><strong>Note:</strong> The nearest station does not always represent your exact location. Whether a station sits next to a busy road or inside a park affects its readings. Looking at several nearby stations gives a more balanced picture.</div>
