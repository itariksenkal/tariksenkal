AI in Legal Practice: Why a System Without Citations Is Useless

TŞ
İsmail Tarık Şenkal
HatsonTech · Co-Founder
· 8 min read· Last updated: 9 August 2026

Law is among the most debated domains for AI — for good reason. In legal work, a fluent answer means nothing; where it came from means everything. A system that cannot show its sources is not a legal tool, however convincingly it writes.

The real problem: verifiability

For a lawyer, text produced by AI is a starting point, not a result. Every sentence that reaches a filing must rest on a checkable basis. What matters in law is therefore not the model but the architecture that ties its answer to a source.

The practical test is simple: when the system asserts something, can it show in one click which decision, which article or which document that assertion rests on?

Why a plain LLM is not enough

A general-purpose language model answers from patterns it remembers. Producing a docket number that does not exist is not technically surprising — the model follows probability, not truth. In law such an error is not merely wrong; it is professionally dangerous.

The basic rule: a legal AI system should not produce a claim it cannot source. Saying "I do not know" is incomparably more valuable than a convincing fabrication.

Meaning-based case-law search

Classical search matches keywords; a lawyer searches for the legal character of a situation. The same legal circumstance can be described in very different words across decisions. RAG architecture and semantic retrieval make the difference here: the query is matched on closeness of meaning rather than overlap of words.

The difficulty of Turkish legal language

Turkish is an agglutinative language, and legal Turkish carries its own terminology. Models designed around English lose efficiency at the tokenisation stage alone; legal terms fragment and meaning disperses. Domain-adapted models and field-specific embeddings close that gap.

Confidentiality and responsibility

RequirementWhy
Data isolationFile contents belong to the client
Source attributionAuditability
Human approvalFinal responsibility rests with the lawyer
Logging and traceabilityWhich output rested on what

The last row matters most: AI is an assistant, not a representative. Judgement and liability remain with the lawyer in every case, and the system should not be designed in a way that weakens that.

Frequently asked questions

Will AI replace lawyers?

No. It shortens research and drafting time; it does not take over legal judgement, choice of strategy, or responsibility.

How is the risk of fabricated decisions reduced?

By grounding answers strictly in retrieved documents, pairing every claim with a source, and refusing to answer when no source is found.

Does client data reach the model?

That depends on the architecture. For enterprise use, data isolation, retention limits and a guarantee of non-use in training should be settled contractually.

The value of AI in law is not speed but auditable speed. Grounded generation is not a preference in this field; it is a requirement. For more, see RAG architecture and reducing hallucinations.
